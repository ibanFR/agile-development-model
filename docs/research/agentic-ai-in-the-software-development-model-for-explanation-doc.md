# Research: Agentic AI in the Software Development Model (input for an explanation document)

**Date**: 2026-09-30 | **Researcher**: nw-researcher (Nova) | **Confidence**: Medium-High | **Sources**: 60 (26 new, 34 reused from the XP research; average reputation 0.89)

## Executive Summary

Agentic AI fits into the Software Development Model as a tool *inside* its components, not as a new participant or a replacement for any focus area. Across all five focus areas the evidence shows the same pattern (X1). Agents are good at turning an agreed decision into an artefact: drafting user stories, formulating Gherkin, generating acceptance tests, drafting a glossary or first-cut domain model, writing code against tests, and classifying feedback. They are weak at reaching the decision. User stories come out fluent but less independent and less diverse (AU1). Domain models come out with good vocabulary but missing relationships, and errors compound in later design steps (DDD1). Gherkin comes out relevant and clear, but with omissions and hallucinations (BDD1). In `workspace.dsl` terms, agents speed up the component-to-component relationships ("formulate", "automate", "apply tactical patterns", "write just enough code"). The person-to-component relationships ("presents rules and examples", "validates and categorizes subdomains", "validates product increments") are where the model needs humans.

What stays is the collaboration between the Domain Expert and the Software Engineer, and the evidence gives a reason why. Domain knowledge, not modelling skill, is what corrects an LLM's model. People also anchor on LLM drafts: novices adopted about two-thirds of an LLM draft's incorrect classes (DDD2, X2). BDD's creators define it as a conversation, not a testing tool, which makes generated scenarios a risk to the "creates shared understanding" relationship if they replace the Specification Workshop rather than feed it (BDD2). What changes is where effort goes. The Domain Expert gains leverage and load, because cheap building makes product judgement and a supply of small, well-formed backlog items the constraint (AU2, L1). The Software Engineer's weight shifts from implementing towards specifying and verifying (X3). The XP control loop, the Acceptance Tests that guide implementation, and Lean's short iterations and informative workspaces become the harness that keeps faster output aligned and stable (XP1, L1, L2). Agent instruction files join the Knowledge Base as a new kind of team knowledge. They are followed, but they do not generally raise task success, so their value lies in team-specific knowledge rather than generic overviews (L3).

The central tension for the explanation document is speed versus shared understanding (X4). Evidence for each focus area comes mostly from studies of single activities, often with students or offline datasets. No study was found of a product team using agents across the whole model, of Example Mapping with an LLM, or of Event Storming with agents in a live team (Knowledge Gaps). The mapping onto the model is therefore interpretation built on activity-level evidence, and is labelled as such throughout.

## Research Methodology

**Scope and purpose**: input for an explanation-type document (Diátaxis/DIVIO) on how agentic AI fits into the Software Development Model described by `workspace.dsl` and `CONTEXT.md`. The emphasis is on the "why": rationale, trade-offs, what changes, what stays, and tensions. It is not a how-to.

**Reuse and extend**: the earlier XP research (`docs/research/xp/agentic-development-in-xp-comprehensive-research.md`, sources S1–S69) already covers XP practices, DORA, METR, TDD with agents, pairing and ensembles, CI, small batches and cognitive debt. **Search strategy**: web searches and page fetches on arXiv (requirements engineering with LLMs, user-story generation, Gherkin generation, acceptance-test generation, LLM domain modelling, Domain-Driven Design with LLMs, context files for coding agents, feedback analysis), the Thoughtworks Technology Radar, martinfowler.com, InfoQ, cucumber.io and the originating authors of product discovery (SVPG). ## Findings

### Align and Understand

The focus area's components are Product Brief, Domain Discovery (User Story Map, Event Storming) and Product Backlog. The Domain Expert "presents Product Feature" to the Product Brief; the Software Engineer "obtains domain knowledge" from it; Domain Discovery "creates" the Product Backlog, which feeds the Specification Workshop ("collaborative requirement specification") and Strategic Architecture ("problem decomposition"). The container relationship to DDD is "collaborative domain modelling".

#### Finding AU1: LLMs can draft user stories of comparable surface quality to humans, but with less diversity, less independence and lower acceptance-criteria fit
**Evidence**: Quattrocchi, Pasquale, Spoletini and Baresi replicated an interview-based elicitation experiment with 10 LLMs acting as analysts and one LLM as the customer, producing 13,958 user stories. LLMs matched humans on "coverage and stylistic quality" but showed "lower diversity and creativity" and "meet the acceptance quality criteria less frequently", regardless of model size; LLMs could reliably evaluate semantic quality when given "clear evaluation criteria" [N7]. Sakib et al. generated user stories from more than 1,000 health-app reviews with three LLMs; the best configuration scored slightly above agile experts on readability and related criteria (mean 4.53 against 4.39), but LLM stories consistently scored lower on *independence* (0.26–0.28 against 0.32) and *uniqueness* (0.29–0.31 against 0.33), and the authors recommend "LLM-assisted drafting followed by human refinement" [N8]. The Thoughtworks Radar notes that when spec-driven tools produce PRDs or user stories "it's sometimes unclear who their intended user is" [N5], and Böckeler found a minor bug fix turned into "four user stories with 16 acceptance criteria" (XP research [S35]).
**Sources**: [N7] arXiv 2507.15157; [N8] arXiv 2603.28163; [N5] thoughtworks.com; XP research [S35] martinfowler.com
**Confidence**: Medium-High (two independent academic studies agree on the pattern "fluent but less diverse and less independent"; two practitioner sources agree on over-production). Transfer limit: both academic studies are offline generation tasks, not live backlog refinement with a team.
**Interpretation for the model**: A Product Backlog is valuable because its items are small, independent and ordered by the Domain Expert. The two properties LLM drafts are weakest on (independence, uniqueness) are exactly the ones that make backlog items sliceable and prioritisable. Agents can accelerate the *writing* of backlog items, but the Domain Discovery → "creates" → Product Backlog relationship rests on a conversation about the user journey, and that is not what the studies measured.

#### Finding AU2: When building gets cheap, product discovery and the product person's judgement become the constraint
**Evidence**: Marty Cagan (who coined "product discovery") writes that with AI prototyping tools "10-20 prototypes (or prototype iterations) per week is easy for just about anyone", and that live-data prototypes can be made "dramatically faster and cheaper than ever before"; the hard part is "product sense" to evaluate what is learnt [N9]. He separates "building to learn" from "building to earn": production products must handle "scale, performance, fault tolerance, reliability, accuracy, privacy, security, operations, provisioning, internationalization and more", and he remains sceptical that generation tools will close that gap soon, citing "limitations of spoken language as a specification language" [N10]. At QCon London 2026, Hannah Foxwell reported teams running out of backlog because "developers are now producing features faster than product managers can supply well-qualified work" (XP research [S56]). DORA 2025 warns that without a user-centric focus "AI-assisted development teams may just be moving quickly in the wrong direction" (XP research [S4]).
**Sources**: [N9][N10] svpg.com; XP research [S56] infoq.com; XP research [S4] cloud.google.com (DORA)
**Confidence**: Medium-High (three independent organisations — SVPG, a QCon practitioner, DORA — converge on "direction-setting becomes the bottleneck"; none is a controlled study).
**Interpretation for the model**: The Domain Expert "owns and drives the product vision" (CONTEXT.md). Cheaper building does not reduce that role; it raises its load and its leverage. The Product Brief ("frame and constrain the project") becomes more important as a constraint on what agents are asked to build, and the Domain Expert may use agents to build throw-away prototypes during Domain Discovery (building to learn), while production increments still flow through BDD, DDD and XP (building to earn). Cagan's distinction is a useful vocabulary for the explanation document.

#### Finding AU3: The evidence supports agents as drafting and synthesis aids in Align and Understand, with humans owning elicitation and decisions
**Evidence**: Quattrocchi et al. conclude that human oversight remains valuable "for elicitation activities requiring deeper contextual understanding" [N7]; Sakib et al. recommend hybrid drafting and refinement [N8]; Böckeler notes that spec-driven development leaves unclear "who should perform requirements analysis" (XP research [S35]); Böckeler's autonomy experiment showed agents silently filling business-logic gaps with assumptions (XP research [S14]).
**Sources**: [N7][N8] arXiv; XP research [S35][S14] martinfowler.com
**Confidence**: Medium (two academic and two practitioner sources, none measuring a live product team).
**Interpretation for the model**: The relationship "Product Brief → spread domain knowledge → Domain Discovery" describes knowledge moving between people. An agent that drafts a story map from a brief spreads text, not knowledge; the Software Engineer's "obtains domain knowledge" edge still needs the conversation with the Domain Expert.

### BDD

The focus area's components are Specification Workshop (Example Mapping), Features (Gherkin) and Acceptance Tests (Cucumber). The Domain Expert "presents rules and examples" and the Software Engineer "identifies functional gaps or inconsistencies" in the Specification Workshop; it "formulate[s]" Features, which are "automate[d]" into Acceptance Tests, which "guides code implementation" in Pair Programming. The container relationship BDD → XP is "creates shared understanding".

#### Finding BDD1: LLMs generate syntactically valid, mostly relevant Gherkin and acceptance tests, but with omissions and hallucinations that require human review
**Evidence**: Hassani, Sabetzadeh and Amyot (University of Ottawa) had 10 evaluators assess 60 Gherkin specifications generated by two LLMs from food-safety regulations: 95% were rated mostly or fully relevant, 100% clear, 94.2% complete and 91.7% of assessments reported significant time savings; yet qualitative feedback identified recurring "omissions ... hallucinations ... and mixed intents", and the authors call the outputs "draft artifacts rather than authoritative statements" needing human-in-the-loop review [N3]. Siddeeq, Abbasi, Rasku, Zhang, Christophe, Mikkonen and Abrahamsson (107 requirements from the PURE dataset) found that organising generation by epic improved expert-rated correctness (4.61 against 4.14), executability (4.61 against 4.07) and completeness (4.31 against 3.50) over a naive LLM baseline [N2]. In an industrial case study (AST 2025), Ferreira et al. generated Gherkin from user stories and then Cypress scripts: 95% of scenarios were found helpful, but of the generated test cases only 60% were usable as-is, 8% needed minor fixes, 24% needed regeneration and 8% were discarded; they conclude LLMs help "with appropriate tooling and supervision" [N4].
**Sources**: [N3] arXiv 2508.20744; [N2] arXiv 2607.01980; [N4] arXiv 2504.07244 (IEEE/ACM AST 2025)
**Confidence**: High that generation is useful but needs review (three independent academic groups). Evaluators in [N3] were students and in [N2] the authors' own researchers, which limits the claim about quality.
**Interpretation for the model**: The evidence supports agents in the Specification Workshop → "formulate" → Features and Features → "automate" → Acceptance Tests relationships, i.e. formulation and automation. None of these studies measured the *discovery* conversation itself.

#### Finding BDD2: BDD's value is the conversation, not the Gherkin; generated scenarios risk reproducing the "Cucumber as a testing tool" failure
**Evidence**: Aslak Hellesøy, creator of Cucumber, argued (2014) that "If you think Cucumber is a testing tool ... you are wrong": it "was born out of the frustration with ambiguous requirements and misunderstandings between the people who order the software and those who deliver it"; when teams adopted it only for automation, scenarios became "imperative and lose their documentation value", "slow and brittle"; and "Your cucumber features should drive your implementation, not reflect it" [N6]. The evaluations in BDD1 measure the *artefact* (relevance, clarity, completeness), not whether the people involved share an understanding [N2][N3][N4]. Research on skill formation and comprehension shows that delegating work to AI reduces the user's understanding of the result (XP research [S38][S61]).
**Sources**: [N6] cucumber.io (Cucumber creator, pre-AI); [N2][N3][N4]; XP research [S38][S61] arXiv
**Confidence**: Medium. The "conversation is the point" position is authoritative for BDD's intent; the inference that generated scenarios erode it rests on analogy with the comprehension studies, not on a study of BDD teams. See Knowledge Gaps.
**Interpretation for the model**: The model already encodes this: the Specification Workshop is tagged as a product component, and both people have edges into it ("presents rules and examples", "identifies functional gaps or inconsistencies"). If an agent writes the Features without the workshop, the BDD → XP "creates shared understanding" edge loses its meaning even if the Acceptance Tests still pass. The explanation document can present agent-generated scenarios as an input *to* the workshop (candidate examples, edge cases to discuss), not as its replacement.

#### Finding BDD3: Spec-driven development overlaps with BDD's goal but differs in size, audience and ownership; practitioners are sceptical of heavyweight specs
**Evidence**: The Thoughtworks Radar (ring Assess) defines spec-driven development as workflows that "begin with a structured functional specification, then proceed through multiple steps to break it down", typically spec → plan → implement; it warns "We may be relearning a bitter lesson — that handcrafting detailed rules for AI ultimately doesn't scale" and that some tools produce "lengthy spec files that are hard to review" [N5]. Böckeler found spec-kit output "very verbose and tedious to review", that agents often ignored specs, and drew a parallel with model-driven development (XP research [S35]). A pre-registered evaluation found a *short* (267-word) specification frame reduced defects in five models (XP research [S65]); tests given up-front improve generated code (XP research [S21][S22]). Cagan points to "limitations of spoken language as a specification language" [N10].
**Sources**: [N5] thoughtworks.com; XP research [S35] martinfowler.com; XP research [S65][S21][S22] arXiv; [N10] svpg.com
**Confidence**: Medium-High (independent practitioner sources agree on the critique; academic sources agree small, executable specifications help).
**Interpretation for the model**: BDD in this model already is a form of specification before code, but its specifications are small (one rule, a few examples), agreed with the Domain Expert, and executable. That positions BDD as the model's answer to "what should the agent build", with a different trade-off from spec-driven tools: less text, more conversation, and a test that fails when the behaviour is wrong. This is interpretation; no study compares BDD and spec-driven development directly.

#### Finding BDD4: Acceptance tests are the natural agent specification and the artefact agents must not rewrite (reused)
**Evidence**: Reused from XP research: tests given up-front improve LLM correctness ([S21][S22]); agents exploit tests when they can edit them, and read-only tests reduce cheating to near zero ([S24]); Farley frames trust as coming "from executable specifications and continuous verification" ([S20]) and has long described acceptance tests as executable specifications that complement TDD ([S69]).
**Sources**: XP research [S21][S22][S24][S20][S69]
**Confidence**: High (reused; see XP research A1, A2, F3).
**Interpretation for the model**: The Acceptance Tests → "guides code implementation" → Pair Programming relationship is where the Domain Expert's intent reaches the agent. Protecting these tests from agent edits is how the model keeps the Domain Expert's authority over behaviour.

### DDD

The focus area's components are Strategic Architecture (Core Domain Charts, Context Maps), Software Design (Bounded Context Canvas, Aggregate Design Canvas, C4) and Code the Domain Model (tactical patterns, the "building blocks"). The Domain Expert "validates and categorizes subdomains"; the Software Engineer "identifies strategically significant parts of the domain". Align and Understand → DDD is "collaborative domain modelling".

#### Finding DDD1: LLMs work as a sparring partner for the early, language-level steps of DDD, but errors compound in later design steps
**Evidence**: Eisenreich, Jusic and Wagner (industrial case study against requirements from the FTAPI enterprise platform, 2026) split DDD into five prompted steps: ubiquitous language, simulated Event Storming, bounded contexts, aggregates and technical architecture. "Steps 1 to 3 worked well, but the accumulated errors rendered the artifacts generated from Steps 4 and 5 impractical", because "minor errors or inaccuracies can propagate and accumulate"; glossaries and context maps were the useful outputs. They conclude "LLMs can enhance, but not replace, architectural expertise", acting as "a collaborative sparring partner for building actionable documentation" [N11]. Chen, Yang, Chen, López, Mussbacher and Varró (MODELS 2023) compared GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on ten domain-modelling tasks with expert reference solutions; the best configuration reached F1 of 0.76 for classes, 0.61 for attributes and 0.34 for relationships, with higher precision than recall (what is produced is often right, much is missing), and they judge full automation impractical [N16; verified at search-abstract level only, publisher pages not fetchable]. Chen, Shen and He found that decomposing modelling into sequential sub-questions improved recall and F1 over a single prompt [N17].
**Sources**: [N11] arXiv 2603.26244; [N16] IEEE MODELS 2023; [N17] arXiv 2410.09854
**Confidence**: Medium-High (three independent groups; one industrial case, two benchmark studies; [N16] only partially verified).
**Interpretation for the model**: The pattern "good at vocabulary, weaker at relationships and structure, errors compound downstream" maps onto the DDD chain Strategic Architecture → "collaborative modelling" → Software Design → "apply tactical patterns" → Code the Domain Model. Agents help most at the start (drafting a glossary, candidate events, a first context map) and least where decisions about boundaries and aggregates accumulate. Low recall means an agent-drafted model silently leaves things out, which is the kind of gap the Domain Expert "validates and categorizes subdomains" edge exists to catch.

#### Finding DDD2: Domain knowledge, not modelling skill, is what corrects an LLM's model; and people anchor on what the LLM proposes
**Evidence**: Silva Mercado, Ma, Cabot and colleagues (Software and Systems Modeling, 2026; 67 participants refining five models) found a human-in-the-loop question-and-answer refinement improved LLM-generated domain models "particularly for elements where LLMs typically struggle", and that "Domain knowledge is more critical than modeling expertise for successful refinement" [N18]. Bragilovski, van Can, Dalpiaz and Sturm (Empirical Software Engineering, 2026; 127 students deriving models from user stories) found that an initial ChatGPT model helped class identification in simple projects, but in the complex project the group with no initial model identified classes better; participants incorporated about 85% of the correct classes *and about 67% of the incorrect classes* from the initial model, and the authors warn this reliance "may limit creativity and hinder the deeper reasoning required to develop robust domain modeling skills" [N19]. Lee et al. (CHI 2025) found higher confidence in GenAI associated with less critical thinking (XP research [S40]).
**Sources**: [N18] Springer SoSyM; [N19] Springer EMSE; XP research [S40] ACM CHI
**Confidence**: Medium-High (two peer-reviewed experiments plus a large survey agree; participants were students or university participants, not professional Domain Experts).
**Interpretation for the model**: This is the strongest evidence found for why the model's DDD focus area is described as "a creative collaboration of domain experts and software engineers". An LLM draft shifts the Domain Expert's job from producing a model to correcting one, and correction depends on domain knowledge that only the Domain Expert holds. The anchoring result is a design reason for when to show an agent's draft: after the people have modelled (as a check), not before (as a starting point), especially in complex domains. This ordering is interpretation, not a tested intervention.

#### Finding DDD3: Ubiquitous language and bounded contexts become context for agents; DDD authorities frame DDD as more relevant, not less
**Evidence**: Eric Evans (Explore DDD 2024 keynote, reported by InfoQ) said "a trained language model is a bounded context", that training a model "on a ubiquitous language of a bounded context makes it far more useful for specific needs", and encouraged practitioners to experiment and share results; he framed his remarks as a snapshot in a fast-moving field [N1]. Michael Plöd (INNOQ, author on DDD) framed a 2026 session as "DDD is not the problem of AI. DDD is the answer" and posed open questions: how Event Storming and Domain Storytelling output can guide agentic development, whether context maps need rethinking when agents consume and produce domain logic, and how ubiquitous language works when models "simulate understanding without true comprehension" [N20]. Thoughtworks' "AI-friendly code design" says good design for humans, including expressive naming and modularity, also benefits AI (XP research [S18]); Garg's "Knowledge Priming" and "Encoding Team Standards" patterns make tacit knowledge explicit for agents (XP research [S34]).
**Sources**: [N1] infoq.com; [N20] innoq.com (talk abstract; positions and questions, not findings); XP research [S18] thoughtworks.com; XP research [S34] martinfowler.com
**Confidence**: Medium for "DDD authorities see DDD as more relevant with LLMs" (two named DDD authorities plus two practitioner sources). Low for any claim that this *works*: these are positions and open questions, not evaluations.
**Interpretation for the model**: A glossary such as this repository's CONTEXT.md is the ubiquitous language written down; for agents it is also context. That gives the Software Design component's "naming to responsibilities" work a second audience. The open questions Plöd poses are honest knowledge gaps for the explanation document rather than settled answers.

#### Finding DDD4: Teams keep the domain core human-steered; agent adoption without design steering raises complexity (reused)
**Evidence**: Reused from XP research: the SpareBank 1 mob kept writing domain-layer code themselves "to have full control" and used the agent at the edges ([S57]); Cursor adoption caused persistent rises in static-analysis warnings and code complexity ([S52]); agent code degrades over iterative extension ([S64]); Beck's agent "accumulated so much complexity that the AI completely stalled" ([S10]).
**Sources**: XP research [S57][S52][S64][S10]
**Confidence**: Medium (one practitioner report for the "domain core" choice; High for the complexity trend, see XP research A5, F6).
**Interpretation for the model**: The Code the Domain Model → "guides code implementation" → Pair Programming relationship is where DDD's building blocks (entities, value objects, aggregates) meet the agent. The evidence suggests the building blocks that express core-domain rules are the least suitable for unsteered generation, and generic or supporting subdomains the most. That is a mapping onto Strategic Architecture's core/supporting/generic distinction, and it is interpretation; no study tests it.

### XP (reused)

The XP focus area is covered in depth by the XP research; this section only restates the conclusions the explanation document needs, by reference.

#### Finding XP1: Agents fit inside XP's feedback loops, not in place of its practices (reused)
**Evidence**: Summarised from XP research. Pair Programming: an agent covers the tactical benefits of pairing but not knowledge sharing; the evidence favours a human pair or ensemble navigating with the agent as driver (A3, B2, F2: [S30][S31][S42][S57][S58][S59][S60][S61]). Test-Driven Development: tests up-front improve generated code, but agents game editable tests, so humans own the red step (A1, A2: [S21][S22][S24][S25]). Continuous Integration: DORA associates AI with more throughput *and* more instability, and names small batches and version-control discipline as amplifiers; CI, not the agent's report, is the source of truth for green (A6, C1, C2, F5: [S2][S3][S4][S44][S46]). Shared understanding is the main thing under threat ("cognitive debt", B2: [S17][S38][S40]).
**Sources**: XP research, as listed
**Confidence**: High for the direction (see XP research, which rates most of these High or Medium-High); no controlled study of an XP team as a whole exists (XP research Gap 1).
**Interpretation for the model**: The XP chain Pair Programming → "write just enough code" → Test-Driven Development → "push code to version control" → Continuous Integration → "deliver product increment" → Short Iterations is the control loop that makes agent output shippable. For the whole-model view, XP is where the upstream focus areas (BDD's Acceptance Tests, DDD's Code the Domain Model, both "guide code implementation") meet the agent.

### Lean Product Development

The focus area's components are Informative Workspaces (boards, DORA/SAFe metrics), Short Iterations (one to two weeks), Knowledge Base (merged with Living Documentation, ADR 0002) and Customer Feedback. Short Iterations "visualize progress on" Informative Workspaces, "updates" the Knowledge Base and "gathers" Customer Feedback; the Domain Expert "validates product increments" and the Software Engineer "works in small steps".

#### Finding L1 (Short Iterations, flow): Agents move the constraint from writing code to reviewing it and to supplying well-specified work
**Evidence**: In a longitudinal study of an enterprise "2x" mandate (802 developers, 196,212 PRs), per-capita merged PRs reached 2.09 times the baseline while "per-reviewer load roughly doubled and automated review overtook human review", with merge and revert rates stable (XP research [S54]). DORA's analysis of open-ended responses names a "verification tax" and a review burden from larger AI-generated change sets, and recommends to "enforce small batches" (XP research [S46]). Foxwell reports teams running out of well-qualified backlog (XP research [S56]). Agent success falls steeply with task length: near 100% on tasks of under four minutes of human time, under 10% on tasks over about four hours (XP research [S62]).
**Sources**: XP research [S54] arXiv; [S46] dora.dev; [S56] infoq.com; [S62] arXiv
**Confidence**: Medium-High (independent field study, research programme and practitioner report agree on the shift; the benchmark supports small units of work).
**Interpretation for the model**: Short Iterations are a batch-size and WIP control. With agents, the scarce resources in an iteration are human review, the Domain Expert's validation ("validates product increments") and well-formed backlog items, not typing. That argues for keeping iterations short and increments small rather than filling a two-week iteration with more agent output. It is a Lean argument (optimising a non-constraint does not raise throughput) applied to evidence from the sources above; no source studies iteration length with agents directly.

#### Finding L2 (Informative Workspaces): What the workspace shows must include stability, rework and quality trends, because perceived speed is unreliable
**Evidence**: DORA 2025: AI adoption relates positively to throughput and product performance but negatively to delivery stability (XP research [S44]). METR's RCT found experienced developers 19% slower with AI while believing they were about 20% faster (XP research [S7]). A peer-reviewed difference-in-differences study found a transient velocity gain and persistent rises in static-analysis warnings and code complexity after Cursor adoption (XP research [S52]). DORA 2024 recorded a 7.5% rise in documentation quality alongside a 7.2% fall in delivery stability per 25% rise in AI adoption (XP research [S2]).
**Sources**: XP research [S44][S7][S52][S2]
**Confidence**: High (reused; independent research programme, RCT and peer-reviewed study).
**Interpretation for the model**: The Informative Workspaces component already names "DORA/SAFE metrics". Agents make the choice of what to make visible more consequential: a board that shows only throughput will show improvement while stability, review queues and complexity may be getting worse. The explanation document can present Informative Workspaces as the model's antidote to the perception gap.

#### Finding L3 (Knowledge Base): Agent instruction files are a new, fast-changing kind of team knowledge; their value lies in non-standard, team-specific knowledge rather than repository overviews
**Evidence**: An empirical study of 2,303 agent context files in 1,925 repositories found they behave like "configuration code through frequent, small additions" rather than static documentation; they mostly cover test procedures (75.9%), implementation details (70.8%) and architecture (68.1%), and rarely security (14.8%) or performance (14.5%) [N12]. Gloaguen et al. found that "providing context files does not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average", that "repository overviews ... are not helpful", but that "instructions in the context files are well followed", concluding they "are useful for specifying non-standard coding practices" [N13]. A two-agent ablation on 17 real tasks found no measurable correctness effect of context strategy [N14]. Thoughtworks places "Curated shared instructions for software teams" in Adopt: "As soon as a prompt is refined, the entire team benefits" [N15]; Garg's "Encoding Team Standards" and "Context Anchoring" patterns make tacit knowledge explicit and keep decision records alive for agents (XP research [S34]).
**Sources**: [N12][N13][N14] arXiv; [N15] thoughtworks.com; XP research [S34] martinfowler.com
**Confidence**: High that context files are widely used and followed; Medium-High that they do not generally raise task success (two independent studies, one small); Medium on what content helps (one study's recommendation).
**Conflict**: Practitioner recommendation (Adopt [N15]) and measured task-success effect (null [N13][N14]) differ; see Conflicting Information.
**Interpretation for the model**: ADR 0002 merged Knowledge Base and Living Documentation because both are team knowledge. Agent instruction files and glossaries extend the same idea to a new reader: the agent. The evidence suggests the valuable entries are those only the team knows (conventions, domain terms, decisions), which is the Knowledge Base's purpose, while generic overviews add cost without benefit. Short Iterations "updates" the Knowledge Base; the "frequent, small additions" pattern fits that relationship.

#### Finding L4 (Customer Feedback): LLMs can classify and summarise feedback at scale with moderate accuracy, but interpretation and prioritisation remain human work
**Evidence**: Mallya, Ferrari, Zadenoori and Dąbrowski evaluated five lightweight open-source LLMs on online user feedback: "moderate-to-high classification accuracy (F1 ~ 0.47-0.68)" and specification quality around 3 out of 5 in human evaluation [N21]. Sakib et al. found LLMs turned app reviews into readable user stories but with lower independence and uniqueness than expert-written ones [N8]. Martinez Montes, Feldt and colleagues, evaluating LLM-assisted reflexive thematic analysis for software engineering research, found evaluators preferred LLM-generated codes 61% of the time, but LLMs fragmented data, overlooked "deeper interpretive layers" and produced imprecisely defined themes, so they are "analytical assistants requiring expert oversight" [N22]. DORA names user-centric focus as an amplifier of AI benefit (XP research [S4]).
**Sources**: [N21][N8][N22] arXiv; XP research [S4] cloud.google.com
**Confidence**: Medium (three independent academic studies; offline datasets, not a product team's feedback loop).
**Interpretation for the model**: Short Iterations "gathers" Customer Feedback, and more increments reaching users means more feedback. Agents can make the gathering and first-pass sorting cheaper, but turning feedback into a changed Product Brief or reordered Product Backlog is the Domain Expert's judgement (Finding AU2). The loop Customer Feedback → (implicitly) Align and Understand is not drawn in `workspace.dsl`; agents make that feedback loop faster only if a person closes it.

## Cross-cutting findings

#### Finding X1: Across focus areas, agents help most in transforming an agreed decision into an artefact, and least in reaching the decision
**Evidence**: The pattern recurs across independent studies. Formulation and automation of agreed behaviour work well with review: Gherkin generation [N2][N3], acceptance-test generation [N4], user-story drafting [N7][N8], feedback classification [N21]. Early vocabulary work in DDD works; later decisions about boundaries and aggregates accumulate errors [N11][N16]. Coding the agreed behaviour works when tests exist and humans own them (XP research [S21][S24]). Fowler and Joshi separate "discovering abstractions", which "requires developer control and iteration", from "applying stable abstractions", where LLMs excel, and caution against "driving everything through prompts" [N23].
**Sources**: [N2][N3][N4][N7][N8][N11][N16][N21][N23]; XP research [S21][S24]
**Confidence**: Medium-High (many independent sources point the same way, but each studies one activity; the cross-activity pattern is a synthesis).
**Interpretation for the model**: In `workspace.dsl`, the decision points are mostly the relationships that involve people: "presents Product Feature", "presents rules and examples", "identifies functional gaps or inconsistencies", "validates and categorizes subdomains", "identifies strategically significant parts of the domain", "validates product increments". The transformation steps are mostly component-to-component relationships: "formulate", "automate", "apply tactical patterns", "write just enough code", "push code to version control". A plain reading for the explanation document: agents accelerate the component-to-component edges; the person-to-component edges are where the model needs humans, and where agents are at most drafting aids.

#### Finding X2: Domain knowledge held by people is the non-substitutable input; agents fill gaps in it with plausible assumptions
**Evidence**: In human-LLM domain modelling, "Domain knowledge is more critical than modeling expertise for successful refinement" [N18]. People adopt about two-thirds of an LLM draft's incorrect classes [N19]. Fowler and Joshi: "The domain model and its vocabulary emerge at the intersection of the functional domain and the solution domain. This is why domain experts with a narrow functional focus cannot work in isolation", and without an established vocabulary "the model has no concrete reference points and must guess at meaning" [N23]. Agents silently fill business-logic gaps with assumptions (XP research [S14]). Coding agents harm users' code comprehension (XP research [S61]) and AI use impairs conceptual understanding (XP research [S38]).
**Sources**: [N18][N19] Springer; [N23] martinfowler.com; XP research [S14][S61][S38]
**Confidence**: High (two peer-reviewed experiments, two further academic studies and two practitioner sources agree; the professional-team setting is not directly studied).
**Interpretation for the model**: CONTEXT.md defines the Domain Expert as the person who "brings domain knowledge into the model". This is the role the evidence says cannot be delegated to an agent, and Fowler and Joshi's quotation supports the model's insistence that the Software Engineer is also in the room (the Software Engineer "obtains domain knowledge" and "identifies functional gaps or inconsistencies"). The collaboration between the two roles, not either role alone, is what the agent lacks.

#### Finding X3: Roles shift rather than disappear: the Domain Expert gains leverage and load; the Software Engineer moves toward specifying, steering and verifying
**Evidence**: Domain Expert side: Cagan says product people can build many prototypes a week and that "product sense" is now the hard part [N9]; Foxwell reports product managers unable to supply well-qualified work fast enough (XP research [S56]); DORA warns of "moving quickly in the wrong direction" without user focus (XP research [S4]). Software Engineer side: Farley, "our role will shift toward defining the problem in greater detail rather than hand-crafting the solution" and "verification becomes the bottleneck" (XP research [S20]); Laycock, "We need engineers to understand systems, not diffs" (XP research [S60]); Foxwell argues review must shift left to "specifications, test strategies and architecture" (XP research [S56]). Diaz, Gayoso, Cimminio and Perez frame specifications as "the contract substrate between humans and agents" in a vision paper that explicitly calls itself "a first step ... rather than a validated theory" [N24]. Hoda argues for a "whole of process" vision of agentic software engineering rather than a code-centric one (ICSE 2026 companion) [N25].
**Sources**: [N9] svpg.com; [N24][N25] arXiv; XP research [S56][S4][S20][S60]
**Confidence**: Medium (consistent practitioner and vision-paper positions from independent organisations; no empirical study of role change in a product team).
**Interpretation for the model**: The model's two people and their edges stay; what each spends time on changes. For the Domain Expert, the Product Brief, the Specification Workshop and the validation of increments become more central because they steer faster building. For the Software Engineer, the weight moves from implementing within XP to specifying (BDD, DDD) and verifying (TDD, CI) around the agent. The model does not need a new person for the agent; the agent sits inside components as a tool, which is consistent with CONTEXT.md's rule that terms describe purpose, not tooling.

#### Finding X4: The central tension is speed versus shared understanding, and the model's collaborative components are where it is resolved
**Evidence**: Thoughtworks defines codebase cognitive debt as "the growing gap between a system's implementation and a team's shared understanding of how and why it works" (XP research [S17]). The enterprise mandate study shows review load doubling and moving to automation (XP research [S54]). Diaz et al. describe a "productivity paradox": "as individual productivity increases, team throughput, review capacity, and stability degrade" [N24; vision paper, drawn from grey literature]. Fowler: LLMs are "a non-deterministic abstraction, so I can't just store my prompts in git and know that I'll get the same behavior each time" [N26]. Hellesøy: features "should drive your implementation, not reflect it" [N6].
**Sources**: XP research [S17][S54]; [N24] arXiv; [N26] martinfowler.com; [N6] cucumber.io
**Confidence**: Medium-High that the tension is real (independent industry radar, field study and practitioner positions); Low for any quantified trade-off.
**Interpretation for the model**: Because prompts are not reproducible [N26], the durable artefacts of a team using agents are the ones the model already names: the Product Brief, the Features and Acceptance Tests, the Software Design documents, the code and its tests, and the Knowledge Base. Shared understanding lives in the collaborative components that produce them (Domain Discovery, Specification Workshop, Strategic Architecture's collaborative modelling, Pair Programming). The explanation document can frame the model's collaborative components as the brake that keeps agent speed aligned with what the team understands, and its automated components (Acceptance Tests, TDD, CI) as the harness that keeps agent output honest.

## Conflicting Information

### Conflict 1: Do agent instruction files help?
**Position A**: Yes, adopt them — Thoughtworks places curated shared instructions in Adopt: "As soon as a prompt is refined, the entire team benefits" [N15] (0.8); Garg recommends encoding team standards (XP research [S34], 0.8); developers maintain them like configuration code [N12] (1.0).
**Position B**: They do not generally raise task success — "providing context files does not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20%" [N13] (1.0); no measurable correctness effect in a two-agent ablation [N14] (1.0, single author, 17 tasks).
**Assessment**: Not fully contradictory. [N13] also finds instructions "are well followed" and useful "for specifying non-standard coding practices"; Position A is about consistency across a team, Position B about benchmark task success. For the explanation document: instruction files are a way to share team-specific knowledge (the Knowledge Base's purpose), not a performance lever, and generic overviews are waste.

### Conflict 2: How good are LLM-generated requirements artefacts?
**Position A**: As good as or better than humans — LLM stories matched or exceeded expert stories on readability-type criteria [N8]; 95% of generated Gherkin rated relevant and 100% clear [N3]; 95% of generated scenarios found helpful [N4].
**Position B**: Systematically weaker on what matters for agile backlogs — lower diversity, lower acceptance-criteria fit [N7]; lower independence and uniqueness [N8]; omissions, hallucinations and mixed intents [N3]; only 60% of generated tests usable as-is [N4]; relationships F1 0.34 in domain models [N16].
**Assessment**: Both hold, and often in the same paper. Surface quality (fluency, format, relevance) is high; structural quality (independence, completeness of relationships, absence of invented detail) is weaker. Evaluator populations (students, the authors' own researchers) limit both positions.

### Conflict 3: Does an LLM draft help people model a domain?
**Position A**: Yes — an initial model improved class identification in simple projects and relationship accuracy in both [N19]; human-in-the-loop refinement improves LLM models [N18]; early DDD steps "worked well" [N11].
**Position B**: It can hurt — in the complex project, the group without an initial model identified classes better, and participants adopted about 67% of incorrect classes [N19]; later DDD steps became "impractical" [N11].
**Assessment**: The same studies support both, and the difference is task complexity and position in the process. Drafts help on simple tasks and early steps; they anchor on complex ones.

### Carried over
Productivity (faster or slower), AI code quality and security, and burnout effects remain contested; see XP research Conflicts 1, 2 and 4.
